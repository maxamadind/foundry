## Why Strength Matters

Strength training — moving progressively heavier resistance over time — is one
of the highest-return habits in health. Muscle is metabolically active tissue:
it improves insulin sensitivity, supports bone density, protects joints, and
makes daily life easier. Adults lose 3–8% of muscle mass per decade after age
30 unless they train against it. The good news: beginners gain strength
rapidly, often adding weight to the bar every session for the first 3–6 months.
This phase is called "novice linear progression," and it is the fastest
progress you will ever make — don't waste it on random workouts.

## The Core Principles

### Progressive Overload

Muscles adapt only when asked to do slightly more than they are used to.
Progressive overload means increasing the demand in small, planned steps: add a
little weight, an extra rep, or another set. A practical rule: when you can
complete all prescribed sets and reps with good form, add the smallest
available increment next session (usually 2.5–5 lb on upper-body lifts, 5–10 lb
on lower-body lifts). Progress is measured in months, not workouts — plateaus
are normal and broken by patience, food, and sleep, not by program-hopping.

### Specificity

You get better at what you practice. If your goal is a stronger squat, you must
squat — leg presses and lunges help, but nothing replaces the movement itself.
Choose 3–5 core lifts, practice them 2–3 times per week, and judge the program
by whether those lifts go up. Assistance exercises support the main lifts;
they don't replace them.

### Form First, Weight Second

Every rep should look the same: controlled, full range of motion, braced trunk.
If adding weight breaks your form, the weight is too heavy. Film your sets from
the side every few weeks — the camera is the most honest coach available. Common
form faults (rounded lower back in the deadlift, knees caving in the squat,
bouncing the bar off the chest in the bench press) are injury risks that get
worse under load, so fix them light before going heavy.

## The Foundational Lifts

Five compound movements cover nearly the whole body. Learn them in this order:

**The Goblet Squat.** Hold a dumbbell or kettlebell at your chest, feet
shoulder-width apart, toes slightly out. Sit down between your heels while
keeping your chest up and heels flat. The counterweight teaches upright
posture automatically, which is why it comes before the barbell squat.

**The Romanian Deadlift.** With a light barbell or dumbbells, hinge at the hips
with soft knees, sliding the weight down your thighs until you feel a deep
hamstring stretch, then drive the hips forward. This teaches the hip hinge —
the single most important movement pattern for back safety — before you ever
pull heavy from the floor.

**The Push-Up.** Hands under shoulders, body in a straight line from head to
heels, lower until your chest nearly touches the floor. Elevate your hands on a
bench if full push-ups are too hard; never sacrifice the straight-body line.
It builds the pressing muscles and trunk stability together.

**The Dumbbell Row.** One knee and hand on a bench, back flat, row the
dumbbell to your hip — not your shoulder — squeezing the shoulder blade at the
top. This balances all the pushing and protects the shoulders.

**The Overhead Press.** Standing, glutes squeezed, press the weight from
shoulder height to full lockout overhead, moving your head slightly back and
then through as the bar passes. Start light: the shoulders are mobile and
vulnerable, and strict form matters more here than anywhere else.

## How to Structure a Week

Train three days per week on non-consecutive days (e.g., Monday, Wednesday,
Friday). Each session: warm up 5–10 minutes, perform 3–4 compound lifts for
3–5 sets each, finish in 45–60 minutes. Alternate two full-body sessions:

**Session A:** Goblet squat, push-up, dumbbell row.
**Session B:** Romanian deadlift, overhead press, dumbbell row.

Rest 2–3 minutes between heavy sets; 60–90 seconds is enough for lighter
assistance work. When Session A's squat stalls for two weeks, swap in a
variation (e.g., front squat); when everything stalls and sleep and food are
handled, take a deload week at 60% of normal weights, then resume.

## Sets, Reps, and Rest

Rep ranges bias the adaptation: low reps build maximal strength, moderate reps
build muscle size, high reps build endurance. Beginners should live mostly in
the 5–8 rep range — heavy enough to practice real strength, light enough to
learn form safely.

| Goal | Reps per set | Sets per lift | Rest between sets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maximal strength | 3–5 | 4–5 | 3–5 minutes |
| Muscle growth | 6–12 | 3–4 | 90 seconds – 2 minutes |
| Muscular endurance | 12–20 | 2–3 | 60–90 seconds |
| Beginner foundation | 5–8 | 3–5 | 2–3 minutes |

Pick a lane for 8–12 weeks before judging it. Mixing rep ranges randomly each
workout trains nothing in particular.

## Eating to Support Training

Training is the stimulus; food is the material. Protein is the priority: aim
for roughly 0.7–1.0 grams per pound of body weight per day, spread across 3–4
meals, from whole foods first (eggs, meat, fish, dairy, legumes). Total
calories decide whether you gain or lose weight — to build muscle while
staying lean, eat at maintenance or a slight surplus of 200–300 calories above
it. Carbohydrates fuel hard sessions; don't fear them on training days. Drink
water before you're thirsty, and keep alcohol modest — it measurably impairs
recovery and protein synthesis.

## Recovery: Where Growth Happens

Muscle is built between workouts, not during them. Sleep 7–9 hours: deep sleep
is when growth hormone peaks and tissue repair runs. Keep a consistent bedtime;
one short night measurably weakens the next session. Manage stress — hard
training plus life stress share one recovery budget. Soreness (DOMS) peaks
24–72 hours after new movements and fades as you adapt; it is not a badge of a
good workout, and its absence doesn't mean a session failed. If fatigue
accumulates for two weeks despite food and sleep, you are under-recovering:
cut volume by a third for a week.

## Safety Rules

1. Never max out alone — use safeties, a spotter, or submaximal weights.
2. Pain is information: sharp or joint pain means stop the exercise, not push through.
3. Warm up every session; cold muscles tear more easily.
4. Progress the weight only when form is solid at the current weight.
5. Keep the training area clear and re-rack weights when finished.
